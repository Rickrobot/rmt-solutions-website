HOW IT IS WRITTEN
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Technical-first, deliberately. Someone searching "tandem lift plan" is not
price shopping — they are trying to establish whether the person they are about
to engage actually understands load transfer. Depth is the conversion mechanism
on this page, not calls to action.

The spine of it is the point that most people get wrong: on a two-crane lift
the load share is not fixed. It moves. Mismatched hoist speeds tilt the load
and transfer weight to the lower hook; turning or upending does the same; so
does asymmetric slinging. And an RCI cannot help, because it monitors its own
crane and has no idea the other machine exists.

Sections that no competitor page covers at all:

  - SIDE LOADING. Why ropes going out of plumb puts lateral force into a boom
    designed for in-line load, and why keeping them vertical is a planning
    requirement rather than operator skill.
  - COMPOUNDING GROUND PRESSURES. Two cranes standing close do not load the
    ground independently — the pressure bulbs overlap. Mat sizing has to be
    worked from the combined case.
  - THE ABORT POSITION. What happens if the lift stops with the load
    part-rotated. That is a different loading case from either end of the
    lift, and it is the detail most missing from tandem plans written by
    people who do not do them often.

It also asks the question a crane hire company would not: CAN ONE BIGGER CRANE
DO IT? A tandem lift is always the more complex option, and where a single
larger machine fits and reaches, that is usually safer and cheaper. Saying so
costs nothing and is the kind of thing that makes the rest of the page
credible.

And it says plainly that a contract lift is often the better answer for a
genuinely complex tandem operation — with independent planning positioned for
the cases where it genuinely fits: cranes already arranged, a second opinion
wanted on a crane company's plan, or the tandem lift being one part of a wider
steel package.


FACTUAL BASIS — CHECKED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - BS 7121-1: where all the factors cannot be accurately evaluated, an
    appropriate down-rating should be applied to all cranes involved —
    commonly 20% or more in practice.
  - LOLER 1998 ACOP (L113): a written plan should exist where two or more
    items of lifting equipment are used simultaneously. This is the strongest
    single line on the page and it is cited as such.
  - BS 7121 treats multiple-crane lifting as a complex lift.

Load-share allowances beyond the two-crane case are described as common
planning practice rather than as a UK standard requirement, because the
numeric factors in circulation (2 cranes +20%, 3 +33%, 4+ +50%) come from
Australian and New Zealand guidance, not BS 7121. The page is worded to reflect
that distinction.
